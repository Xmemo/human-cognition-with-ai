# Human Cognition with AI

**A bilingual research observatory on human cognition in the age of AI.**

As memory, search, writing, reasoning, and judgment become increasingly externalized to AI, the key question is no longer simply whether AI makes us “smarter” or “dumber.” We track three deeper questions:

**How is AI changing human cognition?**  
**How can AI augment rather than substitute for cognition?**  
**What happens when these changes propagate through groups and culture?**

## Latest Research

### Full Research Refresh · 2026-08-29

A full rescan of all nine radars using the current three-layer retrieval system surfaced four signals worth highlighting:

**1｜The separation between “better with AI” and “stronger after AI” now has more direct evidence.**  
A new 28 August writing study added a supervised no-AI near-transfer task: open AI collaboration performed best while AI was present, but bounded support with compulsory reflection performed better in subsequent no-AI writing. An earlier PNAS randomized field experiment shows the same structural distinction. `R1 · R2 · R3 · R4`

**2｜Cross-domain evidence increasingly points to interaction architecture rather than raw AI-use intensity.**  
`human-led`, `bounded support`, `draft-first`, reflection, and verification more often preserve independent performance, ownership, or diversity; `model-led rewrite`, passive copying, and open delegation more often introduce trade-offs. `R1 · R3 · R4 · R6`

**3｜Expertise is not simply flattened by AI, while Control and agency are becoming more measurable.**  
Professional artists retain advantages when using generative AI, and a new critical-AI-use measure operationalizes Verification, Motivation, and Reflection. `R1 · R2 · R3 · R5 · R6`

**4｜The Variance story must move from “AI homogenizes” to a conditional model.**  
Model-led co-creation can reduce idea diversity, yet hybrid collective-search experiments show that human–AI groups can preserve diversity while improving performance. `R3 · R5 · R8 · R9`

## Current Findings

### What we currently know — Baseline 2026-08-29

The evidence does not support a one-dimensional “AI helps cognition” or “AI harms cognition” story. Five higher-level findings currently organize the project.

### 01｜Assisted performance is not retained capability

A person can produce a better answer while AI is present without acquiring the underlying capability. Learning, transfer, reconstruction, and independent performance after AI removal are distinct outcomes and should be measured separately.

### 02｜What gets delegated matters more than how much AI is used

Cognitive offloading is not a single variable. Delegating search or low-value mechanical work is different from delegating problem framing, verification, judgment, or self-regulation. The important question is **which cognitive operation moved outside the person and which control points remained human-regulated**.

### 03｜Metacognition and epistemic agency are emerging as a control layer

A recurring variable across the literature is whether people can decide when to call AI, calibrate trust, detect uncertainty, verify claims, reject suggestions, and know when not to delegate. These control functions increasingly appear central to whether AI behaves more like augmentation or substitution.

### 04｜Human + AI does not automatically produce synergy

Human–AI collaboration is an empirical outcome, not a default assumption. Meta-analytic evidence shows that combinations of humans and AI can underperform the better of the human or AI alone, with results depending on task type, capability gaps, interface design, and division of labor.

### 05｜Individual cognitive effects can become collective and cultural effects

AI may improve average individual performance while changing the variance, language, norms, and search behavior of populations. Creativity research and Machine Culture experiments make it necessary to ask not only whether one person performs better, but also **what happens to the collective search space when many people use similar AI systems**.

→ **[Read the full 2026-08-29 Evidence Baseline](research/baseline-2026-08-29.en.md)** for evidence grades, caveats, counterevidence, unsupported conclusions, and priority research gaps.

### A working model: Control · Retention · Variance

| Dimension | Core question |
|---|---|
| **Control** | Who controls the critical cognitive nodes—goals, problem framing, verification, judgment, and stopping conditions? |
| **Retention** | What can the person still explain, reconstruct, transfer, and repair after AI is removed? |
| **Variance** | When many people share similar AI systems, how much collective diversity and exploratory search space remains? |

This is a **research-organizing working model, not a fully validated grand theory**. It connects individual cognition to human–AI system design and, eventually, collective and cultural evolution.

## 🔭 Frontier Track: Machine Culture

**Machine Culture** changes the unit of analysis from a person using a tool to humans, machines, networks, and cultural transmission interacting over time. A key empirical anchor is Brinkmann et al. (2026), who showed that under specific conditions machine-discovered problem-solving strategies can be learned, transferred, socially transmitted, and preserved by humans after the machine is removed ([Nature Communications](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-76113-2)).

Iyad Rahwan and collaborators at the Max Planck Institute for Human Development's Center for Humans and Machines remain a standing frontier watch because their work connects machine behaviour, Machine Culture, cultural evolution, social learning, human–AI groups, and AI metacognition.

        ↓

        ↓
Weekly update only OR new dated Baseline

**Consensus is the Evidence Backbone, not the sole freshness source.** Retained Consensus results are fetched before citation and checked against canonical DOI, publisher, or arXiv metadata. Newly published, newly indexed, and newly discovered items are labeled separately so discovery date is never presented as publication date.

## Bilingual Publishing, Citation & Scope

Chinese and English public editions use **one frozen evidence set**. English is edited idiomatically from the same evidence rather than produced through a second independent research pass; paper identities, dates, DOI records, evidence grades, and substantive caveats remain aligned.

For project citation see [`CITATION.cff`](CITATION.cff). Original research curation and commentary are released under **CC BY 4.0** unless a file states otherwise; third-party papers remain under their original licenses. See [`LICENSE`](LICENSE).

This repository is a research observatory, not a claim that every included paper is correct. It does not equate citation count with evidence quality, infer authorship from AI-detector scores, or convert correlational or short-term results into long-term causal claims.
