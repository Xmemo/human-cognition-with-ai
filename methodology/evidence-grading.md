# Evidence Grading

Evidence labels describe **what an item can support**, not whether its conclusion is attractive.

## [A] Strong empirical evidence

Typical examples:

- preregistered or otherwise well-designed randomized / controlled experiments with directly measured cognitive outcomes;
- rigorous systematic reviews or meta-analyses of sufficiently comparable studies;
- large, well-designed observational or longitudinal work when the claim is appropriately non-causal;
- convergent evidence across independent methods.

**Does not automatically qualify:** a paper in a prestigious journal, a large citation count, a very large sample with weak measurement, or a meta-analysis with severe heterogeneity interpreted as a universal effect.

## [B] Promising / moderate evidence

Typical examples:

- empirical studies with meaningful but bounded limitations;
- quasi-experiments;
- small or context-specific experiments;
- strong cross-sectional studies when claims remain associational;
- high-quality preprints with transparent methods;
- systematic reviews whose underlying literature is too heterogeneous for strong causal inference.

**Interpretation rule:** useful for mechanism discovery and hypothesis updating, not for sweeping population-level conclusions.

## [C] Conceptual / exploratory

Typical examples:

- theoretical articles;
- comments, perspectives, essays, or position papers;
- qualitative or very small-N studies used to illuminate mechanisms or lived experience;
- computational or agent-based models whose key result depends on modeled assumptions;
- early research with insufficient external validation.

**Interpretation rule:** a concept may be powerful without being empirically established. Always separate the argument from evidence that the mechanism occurs at meaningful scale in the real world.

## [S] Strategic signal

Typical examples:

- new lab / research program;
- workshop or special issue;
- policy or publishing guideline;
- major institutional report or agenda-setting document;
- substantive keynote / research announcement;
- a new benchmark or dataset that changes what can be studied.

**Interpretation rule:** signals tell us where institutions and researchers are moving. They do not prove the underlying cognitive claim.

## Evidence-claim matching

A grade belongs to an **item and claim pair**. One paper can support a strong narrow claim and a weak broad claim.

Examples:

- An RCT can strongly support “structured guidance improved measured outcomes in this course” while weakly supporting “structured AI prevents long-term deskilling.”
- A large web-corpus study can strongly describe a detected population trend while remaining unable to identify the authorship of any individual page with certainty.
- An agent-based model can rigorously show what follows from its assumptions while remaining exploratory evidence about real cultural evolution.

## Mandatory caveat fields

Every selected weekly item should state at least one relevant limitation:

- study design;
- sample / population;
- task ecology;
- measurement validity;
- follow-up duration;
- publication bias / heterogeneity;
- model assumptions;
- generalizability;
- whether the work is peer reviewed.
